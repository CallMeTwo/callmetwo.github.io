Populations and samples

The population is the group you want to know about; the sample is what you can actually measure, and the link between them is sampling.

Overview

A population is the set of people, encounters, records, or events that a study seeks to describe or inform. A sample is the subset observed. The inferential question is not simply whether the sample is large; it is how the observed units connect to the target. A small probability sample can support population inference when selection is well understood, while a very large convenience sample can remain systematically unrepresentative.

Population statements should identify eligibility, place, calendar time, care setting, and unit of analysis. “Adults with asthma” could mean all adults with asthma in a country, patients diagnosed in primary care, people treated at a specialist center, or people who consented to a clinical trial. Estimates from each group answer different questions. The source population, sampling frame, recruited sample, analyzed sample, and population where results may be used should therefore be distinguished explicitly.

The chain from target to observed sample

The target population is the group to which a descriptive estimate or scientific conclusion is meant to apply. The source population is the group that could in principle have generated the participants. A sampling frame operationalizes access to that source, such as a registry or list of clinic visits. Recruitment and consent select participants from the frame; eligibility exclusions and missing outcomes may select again before analysis. Every transition can alter representation.

Suppose investigators estimate 30-day readmission after discharge. If they enroll only patients discharged from a tertiary hospital, exclude people transferred to other facilities, and lose some patients whose readmissions occur elsewhere, their analytic sample is not automatically all people discharged in the region. The denominator and follow-up mechanism determine what the estimated proportion represents. Linkage to regional records may reduce missed events, but linked data can still omit patients who move or use unconnected facilities.

For a finite population such as all admissions to a hospital during 2025, the target may be that actual set of admissions. For a superpopulation target, the interest may be future admissions generated by a continuing process. The finite target can be enumerated and described; superpopulation inference requires a model or sampling argument about future cases. State which one matters, especially when applying finite-population corrections or claiming broad generalizability.

Probability sampling and inclusion chances

In probability sampling, every eligible unit has a known, positive chance of selection. Simple random sampling gives units equal selection probabilities. Stratified sampling divides the frame into meaningful groups and samples within each; it can ensure enough observations from small but important subgroups and improve precision when strata predict outcomes. Cluster sampling selects groups such as clinics, then samples or includes people within selected groups. Multistage designs combine these steps.

Unequal selection probabilities require weighted estimation when the goal is a population average or total. If rural residents are oversampled to make subgroup estimates precise, the unweighted overall estimate overrepresents rural residents. An inverse-probability weight is proportional to 1/πᵢ, where πᵢ is the unit’s inclusion probability. A weighted mean is Σwᵢyᵢ/Σwᵢ. Weights restore the intended composition under the design, but can increase variance when they vary widely. Report the design and variance method rather than treating weighted observations as ordinary independent records.

A worked calculation: a population has 8,000 urban and 2,000 rural adults. A survey samples 400 urban adults and 400 rural adults. If 40 urban respondents and 80 rural respondents report the outcome, the unweighted prevalence is 120/800 = 15%. Yet urban and rural sample fractions are 0.05 and 0.20. The population-weighted prevalence is 0.8(40/400)+0.2(80/400)=0.12, or 12%. Oversampling improved precision for the rural estimate, but weighting is needed for the population summary.

survey::svydesign(ids = ~1, strata = ~stratum,
                  weights = ~sampling_weight, data = dat) |>
  survey::svymean(~outcome, na.rm = TRUE)

In practice, retain primary sampling units, strata, finite-population information where relevant, and nonresponse adjustments in the design object. The simple ids = ~1 form above is only appropriate when sampled units are independent within the design; clustered samples require cluster identifiers. A standard weighted average with an ordinary standard error may be badly misleading.

Selection, coverage, and nonresponse

Coverage error occurs when the frame omits part of the target or includes ineligible units. A clinic registry may exclude people who receive care elsewhere; an online survey may underrepresent people without reliable internet access. Increasing the sample size from the same incomplete frame makes the estimate more precise for the frame-covered group, not necessarily for the target population.

Nonresponse can be unit-level (a person does not participate) or item-level (a participant skips a question). If participation is unrelated to the outcome after conditioning on variables available for adjustment, weighting or standardization may reduce bias. If response depends on unobserved health status even after adjustment, estimates may remain biased. Compare respondents and nonrespondents on frame variables, describe follow-up attempts, and consider sensitivity analyses over plausible outcome differences among those not observed.

Consent itself may select participants. People with severe illness, limited time, language barriers, mistrust, or unstable housing may be less likely to enroll. A trial can remain internally valid for its randomized participants while its average treatment effect transports imperfectly to those groups. External validity is not a property granted by large sample size; it depends on whether relevant effect modifiers and care contexts are represented or can be modeled.

Selection bias can also arise from conditioning on a variable caused by both exposure and outcome. For example, a study restricted to hospitalized patients may create an association between a treatment and an unmeasured cause of hospitalization even when none existed in the source population. This collider mechanism is distinct from ordinary confounding. A recruitment diagram and a causal diagram can help locate where selection occurs and clarify which population each comparison describes.

Clustered populations and effective information

People recruited within one hospital often share clinicians, protocols, geography, and referral patterns. Their outcomes are correlated. Treating them as independent makes standard errors too small. If clusters have average size m and intraclass correlation ρ, the approximate design effect is 1+(m−1)ρ. With 20 people per clinic and ρ=0.05, the design effect is 1.95; 1,000 records provide roughly the precision of 1,000/1.95 ≈ 513 independent records under the approximation.

The number of independent clusters can be more important than the number of people for a cluster-level exposure. A program introduced in six hospitals has six independently assigned units, even if thousands of patient records are measured. Small-cluster corrections, randomization-based inference, or carefully specified hierarchical models may be needed. Report clusters, cluster sizes, and whether treatment or exposure varies within or between clusters.

Cluster sampling also affects the estimand. A patient-average estimate weights large clinics more because they contribute more patients. A clinic-average estimate gives each clinic equal weight. The latter may be relevant if policy decisions are made for the typical clinic. These are both legitimate but distinct targets; the weighting choice should follow the question.

Transporting an estimate to another setting

Generalization asks whether a result applies beyond the study participants. Transportability asks whether it applies to a specified target population, often with measured differences in case mix, treatment delivery, or baseline risk. A trial conducted in academic centers may estimate an internally valid effect but have limited evidence for community hospitals. Compare eligibility, age, comorbidities, access, co-interventions, outcome ascertainment, and calendar period.

If effect modifiers are measured in both study and target populations, standardization can reweight study-specific conditional effects to the target distribution. This requires adequate overlap: for each target subgroup, there must be relevant study data and the effect model must be credible. Extrapolating to groups absent from the trial is not solved by regression adjustment. Present overlap diagnostics and sensitivity to model choices.

Baseline risk differences can change absolute benefit even when a relative effect is stable. If a treatment reduces risk by 25%, a target population with baseline risk 20% has an absolute reduction of about 5 percentage points; a population with baseline risk 4% has an absolute reduction near 1 point. This calculation assumes the relative effect transports and should not be mistaken for evidence that it does. Decisions may also change when adverse effects, costs, adherence, or care pathways differ.

Calendar time matters. A sample from before a diagnostic technology or treatment guideline changed may not represent current patients. Changes in coding, referral, screening, and background therapy alter who enters the dataset and how outcomes are observed. Temporal validation can reveal such differences, while periodic recalibration or renewed sampling may be necessary after deployment.

A practical target-population example

Imagine a prediction model developed from 5,000 patients in one urban hospital, all of whom had a complete laboratory panel. The proposed use is to prioritize follow-up for all primary-care patients with diabetes in the region. Before applying the model, compare the development and target populations by age, disease severity, laboratory access, and outcome follow-up. Patients lacking the required panel may be systematically poorer or less connected to care. They are not simply missing rows; they may fall outside the model’s support.

An evaluation sample should be drawn from the intended primary-care setting, with follow-up capable of capturing outcomes whether or not patients return to the original clinic. Assess discrimination and calibration overall and in groups relevant to implementation. If the model systematically underpredicts risk for patients with sparse visits, recalibration may address baseline-risk differences, but it will not fix poor predictor measurement or a changed outcome mechanism. The decision to deploy depends on both population fit and the consequences of errors.

A population claim can therefore be written as a chain: the frame covered these eligible people; this recruitment process yielded the analyzed sample; these assumptions support the estimate for a stated population; and these differences limit transport elsewhere. Naming each step makes a limitation actionable. It can suggest adding sites, improving linkage, sampling underrepresented groups, or narrowing the intended use.

Reporting what the sample represents

Describe inclusion and exclusion criteria, recruitment dates, sites, sampling method, response, losses to follow-up, and the number analyzed. For probability samples, provide selection probabilities, strata, clusters, and weights. For convenience or registry samples, state how records entered the frame and which eligible people may be absent. Report demographic and clinical characteristics relevant to the target claim, but do not treat a table of baseline variables as proof of representativeness.

Separate descriptive claims about the observed sample from inferences about a broader population. A confidence interval captures sampling variation only under its design and model; it does not quantify coverage error or unmeasured selection. Discuss these limitations directly. If the intended target population changes, say why the available evidence supports that extension and which assumptions carry it.

Sampling variability is not representativeness

A confidence interval around a sample estimate addresses how much results might vary under repeated sampling from the assumed process. It does not address whether that process selects the right people. A national web poll may have 100,000 responses and a tiny model-based standard error, yet systematically underrepresent people with low internet access or distrust of surveys. Reporting many decimal places cannot compensate for coverage and nonresponse bias.

For probability designs, uncertainty can be estimated from known random selection. For nonprobability samples, analysts often calibrate or rake sample margins to known population totals. This can reduce imbalance on measured variables, but relies on assumptions that response is explainable by those variables and that relevant groups overlap. If young adults with severe illness never enter the online panel, matching age and sex margins does not recover their outcomes. A sensitivity analysis should explore how unmeasured response differences would alter conclusions.

Design effects and effective sample size provide a useful but incomplete summary of precision. A common approximation is n_eff = n/deff, but it does not change the target and can hide poor subgroup support. If one subgroup has few sampled units, its estimate can remain unstable despite a large overall n. Report subgroup denominators and uncertainty rather than only a total sample size.

Positivity and overlap in target comparisons

Transport or adjustment methods require support: people with relevant covariate patterns in the target need comparable observations in the study sample. In a treatment comparison, positivity means each treatment has a nonzero probability at relevant covariate combinations. In transport, the analogous issue is whether target-population profiles are represented in the source data. Near-zero probabilities produce extreme weights and estimates driven by a few observations.

Inspect weight distributions, propensity or participation score overlap, and covariate ranges. Truncating extreme weights can stabilize variance but trades off bias and may alter the effective target. Restricting inference to the region of common support can be more honest than extrapolating, although the resulting target population is narrower. State this restriction in the conclusion. Sensitivity analysis across plausible trimming or overlap definitions makes the dependence visible.

For example, a treatment trial may include few patients over age 85, while the health system intends to use results mainly in that age group. An interaction model can produce an estimated effect, but it is extrapolating if the trial data contain almost no very old participants. A narrow model-based interval may understate this structural uncertainty. Additional recruitment, pragmatic evidence, or explicit limits on the target claim may be needed.

Recruitment as an ethical and scientific process

Who can participate is shaped by consent capacity, language, transportation, clinic hours, insurance, and trust. Excluding groups for convenience may reduce relevance and concentrate burdens among those with easier access to research. Inclusion is not only an equity concern; it determines whether the resulting evidence can inform care for the people who need it. Ethical recruitment should be paired with appropriate support and accessible procedures rather than relaxed protections.

Eligibility criteria should reflect safety and scientific necessity. Broad criteria can improve applicability but may require stratification or careful monitoring; narrow criteria can make a mechanistic effect easier to estimate while limiting clinical generalization. Document reasons for exclusions and compare excluded with included patients using available information. In routine-data studies, define whether an encounter, person, episode, or facility is the sampling unit and avoid counting recurrent records as independent people.

The data source itself can define a selected population. A claims database includes people with recorded covered services, not all people with the disease. A laboratory repository oversamples those who were tested. A referral-center cohort includes people who reached specialist care. Such samples can be valuable for specific questions, but the source mechanism must be reflected in the target statement and analysis.

Choosing a strategy for population inference

Start by drawing a simple flow from target population to frame, recruitment, analyzed observations, and intended use. At each arrow ask who can be lost and whether the selection factors relate to the outcome or exposure. If selection probabilities are known, use design-based estimators. If participation is modeled from observed covariates, consider inverse-probability weighting or standardization and check support. If selection depends on unmeasured factors, quantify plausible bias or collect additional data rather than implying the issue has disappeared.

The analysis unit should align with the claim. To estimate person-level prevalence, deduplicate repeated encounters and define whether a person with multiple records is counted once. To estimate encounter-level event rates, preserve encounters but account for within-person clustering. To estimate hospital performance, distinguish patient-weighted averages from facility-weighted comparisons. These choices influence both the point estimate and its uncertainty.

A useful reporting paragraph might say: “We sampled 30 primary-care practices by region, then randomly selected eligible adults from each practice. Selection weights reflected practice and patient sampling probabilities; variance estimates accounted for practice clustering. The target was adults aged 18–75 receiving primary care in the region during 2025. People without a recorded visit in the sampling frame were not covered, so findings may not describe adults disconnected from care.” This is much more informative than saying merely that the sample was representative.

When possible, compare estimates from complementary sources, such as a probability survey, routine records, and a cohort with active follow-up. Agreement does not prove absence of bias, but discrepancies can reveal differences in case definitions, coverage, or event capture. Triangulation is most useful when each source has distinct strengths and its limitations are documented.

Also report uncertainty from the sampling design separately from qualitative concerns about coverage, nonresponse, or transport.

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval is a random interval that, over repeated samples, covers the true parameter 95% of the time - not a probability statement about this particular interval.

Overview

An interval estimate communicates both a plausible central estimate and its sampling uncertainty. A confidence interval is generated by a procedure whose long-run coverage is specified under a model; it is not a range of values that all remain equally plausible after seeing the data. The interval’s width is informative about precision, while its location describes the estimate. Neither dimension alone tells whether the effect matters clinically.

From standard error to interval

For an approximately normal estimator θ-hat with standard error SE, a two-sided 95% Wald interval is θ-hat ± 1.96 SE. When estimating a mean with unknown population variance in a small sample, replace 1.96 with a t critical value and use s/√n. For ratios such as risk ratios and odds ratios, construct an interval on the log scale and exponentiate endpoints because the raw ratio is asymmetric and bounded below by zero.

A worked example: a treatment reduces systolic pressure by an estimated 4.0 mmHg relative to control, SE 1.8. The normal interval is 4.0 ± 3.53, or 0.47 to 7.53 mmHg reduction (define sign carefully). If the clinically important reduction is 5 mmHg, this interval includes smaller effects and effects exceeding that target. It is evidence of direction under the model, not proof that the benefit reaches the target.

Reading interval width in context

est <- 4.0
se <- 1.8
est + qnorm(c(.025, .975)) * se

# Small-sample interval for a mean from raw observations
x <- c(126, 131, 119, 124, 128, 121, 133, 122)
t.test(x)$conf.int

The first calculation presumes a normal sampling distribution and an already valid SE. The second uses a t interval for independent, identically distributed measurements with finite variance; it does not make convenience sampling representative. A narrow interval may be precisely wrong under selection bias or outcome misclassification.

Why the familiar formula can fail

Wald intervals can have poor coverage for proportions near 0 or 1 and for small samples. Wilson or exact binomial intervals are often preferable. Bootstrap intervals can help for complicated estimators, but resampling must mirror the sampling design: resample clusters when clusters were sampled, and preserve paired units for paired data. For regression, use model-based, robust, profile-likelihood, or bootstrap intervals according to the estimator and assumptions.

Multiplicity and selection also matter. A 95% interval reported after searching many endpoints does not retain 95% simultaneous coverage. If a subgroup was selected because it looked favorable, the conventional interval ignores that selection. Prespecify the primary contrast or use simultaneous intervals when the inferential family requires them.

Reporting the estimand, not just the limits

Give the estimate, interval, units, population, comparison, time point, and method. For example: “At 12 weeks, the adjusted between-group mean difference in pain score (new treatment minus control) was −2.0 points (95% CI −4.1 to 0.1).” Avoid saying an interval “proves no effect” when it crosses zero; evaluate whether the limits exclude clinically important benefit or harm. Equivalence requires prespecified margins and an interval wholly inside them.

Coverage is a property of the procedure

Imagine repeating the same study many times under the same population model, with each repetition producing a new confidence interval by the same recipe. A nominal 95% method should cover the fixed target parameter in 95% of those repetitions. The unknown parameter is not random in this interpretation; the data and interval are. After one interval is calculated, frequentist coverage does not mean a 95% posterior chance that the parameter lies between those particular endpoints. A Bayesian credible interval makes a different probability statement conditional on its model and prior.

Coverage depends on more than the formula printed by software. It can fail when the sampling frame is unrepresentative, the outcome model is wrong, the variance estimator ignores clustering, or the analysis was selected after searching many outcomes. A “95% CI” label should never imply that these design errors have been handled.

Nonlinear scales and boundary parameters

For a risk ratio or odds ratio, estimate log(θ), calculate its interval on that approximately symmetric scale, then exponentiate both endpoints. Suppose log risk ratio is −0.40 with SE .18. A 95% interval on the log scale is −0.40±1.96(.18), or −0.753 to −0.047; exponentiating gives a risk ratio of .67 (95% CI .47 to .95). This interval is asymmetric around .67 on the ratio scale, as it should be. Wald intervals for proportions near zero or one may extend outside the permissible range; Wilson, score, likelihood, or exact intervals better respect the parameter space in many small-sample settings.

Resampling without breaking the design

Bootstrap intervals use repeated resampling to approximate an estimator’s sampling distribution. The resampling unit must match the independent unit in the design. For paired measurements, resample people with both measurements together. For cluster-randomized trials, resample clusters rather than individuals. For a stratified sample, preserve strata or use an appropriate design-based variance estimator. An ordinary row bootstrap can destroy the dependence structure and produce falsely narrow intervals.

set.seed(41)
x <- c(4.1, 3.8, 5.2, 4.7, 3.9, 4.3, 5.0, 4.5)
# Illustrative percentile bootstrap interval for the mean
boot_means <- replicate(5000, mean(sample(x, replace = TRUE)))
quantile(boot_means, c(.025, .975))

With a small sample, this interval reflects the empirical distribution’s limitations and may perform poorly, especially for skewed estimators or boundary values. More bootstrap replicates reduce Monte Carlo error but do not cure a poor resampling scheme. Bias-corrected or studentized intervals can improve some settings; they should be selected for reasoned statistical properties, not because their endpoints are more attractive.

Interval width as a design quantity

Precision planning can begin from a desired half-width. For a mean with anticipated SD σ and normal critical value z, approximate n=(zσ/h)² for half-width h. If σ=12 and a 95% half-width of 3 units is desired, n≈(1.96×12/3)²≈62. This ignores finite degrees of freedom, attrition, clustering, multiplicity, and uncertainty in σ, so it is a starting point rather than a final design. For a proportion, precision varies with the anticipated rate and is largest near .5 under a simple binomial model.

Simultaneous and selected intervals

A collection of separate 95% intervals does not usually provide 95% coverage for the whole collection. When decisions depend on all pairwise contrasts, use simultaneous intervals such as Tukey’s method; for several treatments versus a control, Dunnett intervals can be more efficient. Repeated interim looks and adaptive selection similarly require procedures calibrated for the planned design. A post hoc interval for a subgroup selected because its estimate was extreme is likely too optimistic. Pre-specify primary contrasts, and label exploratory intervals honestly.

Reporting beyond endpoints

An interval should be accompanied by the estimator, target population, denominator or sample size, outcome scale, covariate adjustment, and method. Give sufficient digits to preserve decision-relevant distinctions but do not imply unwarranted precision. State whether the endpoints are compatible with clinically important benefit and harm. Crossing zero is not a synonym for “no difference”; an interval that rules out both meaningful benefit and harm can be informative even when the corresponding null test is not the centerpiece. For equivalence, show the prespecified margins and confirm the entire interval lies inside them.

Comparing intervals is not a test of interaction

A common visual mistake is to compare whether two subgroup confidence intervals overlap and treat overlap as evidence that effects are the same. Interval overlap is not equivalent to a formal test of the difference between estimates. When subgroups are independent, the variance of a difference combines both standard errors; when they share participants or model parameters, covariance must be included. To test effect modification, estimate the interaction contrast directly and report its interval. An estimate significant in one subgroup and nonsignificant in another does not establish that subgroup effects differ.

Covariate-adjusted intervals and marginal targets

Regression intervals often describe a conditional coefficient holding modeled covariates fixed. Clinical decisions may instead concern average outcomes over a target population. Standardization or marginal prediction can estimate population-average risks by predicting each participant under each treatment and averaging. The interval then must incorporate uncertainty in all model coefficients and, ideally, the target covariate distribution. For logistic models, conditional odds ratios differ from marginal odds ratios because of non-collapsibility; the distinction is mathematical, not necessarily evidence of confounding.

When using adjusted means, explain which covariates are included and how predictions were averaged. Avoid presenting an adjusted contrast as universally applicable if the target population differs from the analysis sample. For trials, pre-treatment prognostic covariates can improve precision, but post-randomization variables may introduce bias if conditioned on without a causal rationale.

Missingness and interval validity

A confidence interval quantifies sampling variability around the estimator under the analysis assumptions. It does not automatically include uncertainty due to missing data mechanisms. Complete-case standard errors may be correct for the analyzed cases while the estimate is biased for all randomized participants. Multiple imputation propagates imputation variability if the model is appropriate and the missing-at-random assumption is plausible; sensitivity analyses are needed when missing not at random is credible. The interval should be described as conditional on these assumptions, and the missing-data strategy should appear alongside the estimate.

Repeated looks and confidence sequences

Ordinary confidence intervals computed repeatedly during ongoing data collection do not maintain their nominal coverage as a sequence if investigators stop when a favorable result appears. Group-sequential confidence intervals can account for planned interim looks. Confidence sequences provide time-uniform coverage under specified conditions, allowing valid inspection at arbitrary times. They are wider than fixed-sample intervals early on because they protect against repeated monitoring. These methods are useful in continuous surveillance and adaptive trials but should be selected during design and matched to the data process.

Precision reporting in practice

A useful table distinguishes descriptive spread, inferential precision, and individual prediction. For example, report arm means and SDs, the adjusted mean difference with a 95% confidence interval, and if clinically useful a prediction interval for a future measurement. Avoid confusing “95% CI for average” with “95% range of patients.” State the denominator, time point, adjustment set, and method. If multiple comparisons are made, identify whether intervals are pointwise or simultaneous. Then interpret the limits against a minimal clinically important difference rather than treating exclusion of zero as the sole decision criterion.

A worked ratio calculation in R

A ratio estimate is easiest to understand through its component counts. With 20 events among 200 intervention participants and 30 among 200 controls, the estimated risk ratio is 0.67. The log-scale standard error is approximately √(1/20−1/200+1/30−1/200)=0.278. A 95% interval on the log scale is log(.67)±1.96(.278), then exponentiation gives roughly .39 to 1.16. The interval includes no relative difference (1) as well as appreciable reduction and some increase. A risk-difference interval will communicate absolute changes instead and may be more useful to patients.

risk_ratio_ci <- function(a, n1, c, n0) {
  rr <- (a/n1) / (c/n0)
  se <- sqrt(1/a - 1/n1 + 1/c - 1/n0)
  exp(log(rr) + qnorm(c(.025, .975)) * se)
}
risk_ratio_ci(20, 200, 30, 200)

This simple formula assumes independent binomial groups with nonzero events. Stratification, covariate adjustment, cluster assignment, or sparse counts require more appropriate methods. If the confidence interval crosses the null value 1, do not describe that as evidence of no effect; interpret the range of ratios.

Comparing precision across studies

A narrower interval usually indicates more information, but width comparisons are meaningful only for the same estimand and scale. A large trial can be precise about a biased effect. Different adjustment strategies can target conditional versus marginal quantities. Different follow-up horizons can produce different risks. Meta-analysis should first assess whether studies estimate sufficiently comparable effects; inverse-variance pooling cannot fix incompatible estimands.

Confidence intervals also express sampling precision, not total uncertainty. Uncertainty about measurement validity, selection, unmeasured confounding, model structure, and transport to another population may exceed the width implied by the statistical interval. Describe these limitations separately instead of informally widening intervals without a defined procedure.

Distinguish confidence, prediction, and tolerance intervals

A confidence interval targets an unknown population parameter such as the mean. A prediction interval targets a future individual or observation and includes both parameter uncertainty and individual variability. A tolerance interval aims to cover a specified proportion of a population with a stated confidence level. For a normal population, a 95% confidence interval for the mean can be very narrow even though a 95% prediction interval for one future patient spans many SDs. Clinical reference ranges are closer to population quantiles or tolerance intervals than confidence intervals for a mean.

The target must be explicit. A statement like “95% of patients lie within this interval” is wrong if the reported interval is for the average treatment effect. When communicating prognosis, prediction intervals may be more relevant; when reporting treatment-effect precision, confidence intervals are appropriate.

Equivalence and non-inferiority intervals

An interval can be used with prespecified decision margins. For two-sided equivalence at α=.05, the 90% confidence interval is often compared with lower and upper equivalence bounds because this corresponds to two one-sided tests. Some regulatory or protocol frameworks require particular conventions; follow the design and state them. For non-inferiority, the relevant one-sided confidence bound must stay within the unacceptable margin. Reporting only a p-value against zero obscures the actual question.

Margins should be justified before data collection using clinical importance and preservation of established treatment effect. A wide margin makes success easier but the claim less useful. Always display the estimated difference and interval against the margins, and explain whether the conclusion concerns statistical criteria, clinical similarity, or both.

Rounding and presentation

Round estimates and interval limits consistently and in units meaningful to the audience. Excessive decimals imply false precision; coarse rounding can hide whether a threshold is crossed. State the confidence level, method, and whether the interval is adjusted for multiplicity. If an interval is highly asymmetric, do not force it into an estimate ± symmetric margin. Include sample size and missingness context so readers can judge the information base.

Intervals from hierarchical models

In multilevel analyses, an interval for the overall treatment effect differs from a prediction interval for the effect at a new clinic. The former reflects uncertainty in the grand mean; the latter adds between-clinic heterogeneity. A narrow pooled interval can coexist with wide uncertainty about what an individual new clinic will experience. This distinction matters when policy decisions are decentralized. Report heterogeneity estimates and prediction intervals when the number of sites supports them, while acknowledging that few clusters make variance estimates unstable.

Model diagnostics and sensitivity

Residual plots, influence analysis, and calibration checks can reveal violations that nominal intervals hide. For a linear model, look for changing residual spread, nonlinear mean structure, influential observations, and dependence. Robust SEs can address some variance misspecification but not nonlinear bias or confounding. Bootstrap intervals are useful only if resampling respects independent units. Compare reasonable methods as sensitivity analyses and predefine which one supports the primary claim.

Intervals for proportions with no events

Zero observed events does not imply zero risk. If 0 of n participants experience an event, a rough “rule of three” upper 95% bound is 3/n under a binomial model: with n=100, risk could still be about 3%. Exact Clopper–Pearson intervals have conservative coverage; Wilson intervals are often shorter with good coverage. The appropriate choice depends on the reporting standard and study design. If participants contribute unequal follow-up, a binomial interval ignores person-time and censoring; use survival or rate methods.

x <- 0; n <- 100
binom.test(x, n)$conf.int
prop.test(x, n, correct = FALSE)$conf.int

These intervals assume independent Bernoulli observations and a fixed denominator. In a rare-event safety study, also describe exposure duration and ascertainment; no-event evidence can remain weak when follow-up is short or high-risk patients are underrepresented.

Intervals and censoring

For survival outcomes, Kaplan–Meier confidence bands estimate uncertainty in survival probability over time, while confidence intervals for median survival may be asymmetric or unbounded if the curve never falls below .5. A hazard-ratio interval summarizes a model parameter under proportional hazards. These are different estimands and should not be conflated. Competing risks require cumulative-incidence intervals rather than treating competing events as ordinary independent censoring when estimating cause-specific risk.

Report intervals so readers can act

When the estimate is a risk difference, name the event and time horizon: “At 12 months, treatment reduced hospitalization by 4 percentage points (95% CI 1 to 7 fewer per 100).” This avoids the ambiguous sign of a subtraction. For ratio measures, state the reference group and null value. For adjusted estimates, clarify whether covariates were baseline and how the contrast was standardized. Explain whether the interval is pointwise or simultaneous and whether missing outcomes were imputed or excluded.

The interval’s endpoints are not guaranteed bounds on the truth in this one study. They are outcomes of a method with a coverage property over repetition. Readers can still use them as a calibrated compatibility summary, provided assumptions and selection are transparent. Include the interval method in tables or footnotes and avoid generic labels when specialized methods were used.

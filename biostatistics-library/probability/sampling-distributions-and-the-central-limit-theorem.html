Sampling distributions and the central limit theorem

A sample statistic changes from sample to sample. Its sampling distribution describes that variation, and the central limit theorem explains why averages often become predictable even when individual observations are skewed.

Sampling distributionsStandard errorCentral limit theorem

Three distributions to keep separate

Suppose researchers record length of stay (LOS) for patients admitted to a hospital. Individual stays vary: many patients leave quickly, while a smaller number stay much longer. The population distribution is the distribution of LOS values in the population the study is trying to describe.

A sample of patients gives one sample mean, x̄. A different sample would usually give a slightly different mean. If we repeatedly took samples of the same size and calculated a mean each time, the resulting values would form the sampling distribution of the sample mean.

Keep the objects distinct. The population distribution describes individual patients. A sample is one group of patients. The sampling distribution describes a statistic across many hypothetical repetitions of that sampling process.

The central limit theorem (CLT) concerns this third distribution. It does not claim that patient-level data become normal, and it does not make a single sample look normal. It describes the behavior of a statistic across repeated samples.

Standard error measures sample-to-sample variation

Let individual observations have population mean μ and standard deviation σ. For a random sample of n independent observations, the sample mean is

x̄ = (X₁ + X₂ + ⋯ + Xₙ) / n

The sample mean is centered on the population mean. Its standard deviation across repeated samples is called the standard error (SE):

SE(x̄) = σ / √n

In practice, σ is usually unknown, so we estimate it with the sample standard deviation s:

estimated SE(x̄) = s / √n

Standard deviation describes spread among individual observations. Standard error describes uncertainty in an estimate across samples. Increasing the sample size makes the standard error smaller at a rate of 1 / √n: quadrupling n halves the SE, all else equal.

What the central limit theorem says

When observations are independent and come from the same population with a finite mean and variance, the distribution of the standardized sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution as the sample size grows:

(x̄ − μ) / (σ / √n) → N(0, 1) as n increases

In practical terms, the distribution of sample means becomes more nearly bell-shaped and remains centered at μ. Its spread is the standard error σ / √n. This is why normal-based methods can often describe uncertainty in averages, even when the original observations are not normally distributed.

When is the sample mean exactly normal?

If the population itself is normal, the sample mean has a normal distribution for any sample size. The CLT is useful in the broader case where the population is not normal: it provides an approximation that generally improves with larger samples.

There is no universal “n = 30” rule

How quickly the approximation becomes useful depends on the population shape. A mildly skewed distribution may need fewer observations than a strongly skewed distribution with rare extreme values. Dependence between observations can also change the sampling distribution. Check the study design and data rather than treating a sample-size threshold as a guarantee.

Worked example: average hospital stay

Imagine a hypothetical sample of 12 admissions with recorded lengths of stay, in days. The values are right-skewed: several stays are short and a few are longer.

Hypothetical sample (days)

1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 17

The sample mean is 5.5 days. The sample standard deviation is about 4.58 days, so the estimated standard error is 4.58 / √12 ≈ 1.32 days. The SE describes how much the sample mean would vary across repeated samples of 12 patients under the same sampling process.

This one sample does not show the CLT by itself. The interactive lab demonstrates the repeated-sampling idea. The example instead shows how a sample standard deviation becomes an estimated SE for an observed sample mean.

From standard error to a 95% confidence interval

A confidence interval combines an estimate with its uncertainty. For a mean, an often-used interval is

x̄ ± t* × (s / √n)

For the example, a t-based 95% interval is approximately 2.59 to 8.41 days. The t critical value accounts for estimating the population SD from the sample. Under independent sampling and a normally distributed population, this interval has exact 95% coverage; for a skewed population it is an approximation, and with only 12 observations the long right tail can make that approximation unreliable.

In repeated use under its assumptions, 95% of intervals constructed this way would contain the fixed population mean. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains the mean.

Calculate the SE and 95% interval in R

This code uses the 12 observed values above. It does not simulate repeated samples. The t-based interval is appropriate when observations are independent and the sample mean is reasonably modeled by a t procedure; strong skew at small n deserves caution.

R · standard error and 95% CI
stay_days <- c(1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 17)

n <- length(stay_days)
sample_mean <- mean(stay_days)
sample_sd <- sd(stay_days)
standard_error <- sample_sd / sqrt(n)

t_critical <- qt(0.975, df = n - 1)
ci_95 <- sample_mean + c(-1, 1) * t_critical * standard_error

sample_mean
standard_error
ci_95

For these data, R returns a mean of 5.50 days, an SE of about 1.32 days, and a t-based 95% interval of about 2.59 to 8.41 days. A large-sample normal approximation uses 1.96 in place of the t critical value, but the CLT does not remove concerns about dependence, extreme tails, or a small sample.

The interval uses the sample standard deviation and 11 degrees of freedom.

Assumptions and common mistakes

  • Independence matters. Repeated measures on one patient or clustering within hospitals reduce the effective amount of independent information. Use methods that reflect the design.
  • Finite variance matters. The usual CLT requires a population with a finite variance. Extremely heavy tails can make normal approximations slow or inappropriate.
  • Skew affects convergence. A larger sample often helps, but there is no sample-size cutoff that guarantees a good approximation for every population.
  • The CLT concerns a statistic. It does not say that the observed data, a histogram of patients, or the population itself becomes normal.
  • SE is not SD. The SD describes individual values; the SE describes uncertainty in an estimate across repeated samples.
  • Precision is not protection from bias. A narrow interval cannot repair selection bias, measurement problems, confounding, or an unrepresentative sample.
Practical reading: first identify the statistic and its sampling process. Then ask whether observations are independent, whether the sample size is adequate for the population shape, and whether the interval’s assumptions match the study.

Key takeaways

  • A sampling distribution describes a statistic across repeated samples.
  • The standard error is the standard deviation of that sampling distribution; for a sample mean, it is σ / √n.
  • The CLT explains why sample means tend toward a normal distribution under suitable conditions, even when individual observations are skewed.
  • Use the CLT as an approximation whose quality depends on the population and sampling design, not as a blanket rule.
  • R can calculate a sample mean’s estimated SE and a t-based confidence interval directly from observed data.

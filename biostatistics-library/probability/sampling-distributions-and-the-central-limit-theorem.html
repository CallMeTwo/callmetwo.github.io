Sampling distributions and the central limit theorem

Why sample means are approximately normal, how the standard error shrinks with sample size, and what this means for confidence intervals.

Overview

A sampling distribution describes how a statistic would vary across repeated samples generated by a specified design. The sample mean is random because each sample differs. Its expected value and standard error connect observed data to population quantities and underpin confidence intervals and tests. The central limit theorem (CLT) often makes a standardized sum approximately normal, but it does not guarantee that every small-sample statistic or biased design is valid.

Mean and standard error

For independent observations with mean μ and variance σ², sample mean X̄ has expectation μ and variance σ²/n, so SE=σ/√n. Since σ is usually unknown, estimate with s/√n. If n quadruples, SE halves under the same design and variance. This precision gain differs from reducing individual-level variability.

Central limit theorem

For independent identically distributed variables with finite variance, the standardized sample mean converges toward standard normal as n grows. The required n depends on skewness, tail heaviness, and the statistic. A highly skewed length-of-stay distribution may need more observations for a good normal approximation than a symmetric lab measure. The CLT concerns sampling distributions, not the distribution of raw observations.

Designs and dependence

Clustering changes variance through covariance; unequal sampling, weights, finite populations, stratification, and repeated measurements change standard errors. The independent-observation formula s/√n is not universal. Bootstrap and simulation must reproduce the sampling unit and design. A CLT cannot fix selection bias or confounding.

Practical use

Use sampling-distribution reasoning to understand confidence intervals, tests, and precision planning. Check small-sample and boundary cases with exact, t, permutation, or bootstrap methods as appropriate. Report the estimand, design-aware SE, and assumptions. A standard error quantifies sampling variation under a model, not total scientific uncertainty.

Difference between outcome and estimator distributions

Individual outcomes may be highly skewed while a sample mean has an approximately normal sampling distribution. The CLT concerns the repeated-sample distribution of a suitably standardized sum. This does not mean raw data become normal as n grows. Nor does it fix a biased estimator: if sampling excludes high-risk patients, a precise normal distribution can center on the wrong target.

For a sample proportion, the binomial distribution is exact under independent Bernoulli sampling; a normal approximation uses SE≈√[p(1−p)/n]. Near 0 or 1, normal intervals can leave [0,1] and have poor coverage. Wilson or exact intervals respect the boundary better. For medians and quantiles, asymptotic distributions depend on density near the quantile, not the same simple CLT formula.

Dependence and design effects

If observations are clustered with average size m and intraclass correlation ρ, variance inflation is roughly 1+(m−1)ρ. Repeated measurements also introduce covariance. Stratification can improve precision; unequal weights can reduce effective sample size. Use design-based or model-based variance estimation that reflects the sampling scheme. The iid CLT is not an excuse to treat correlated records as independent.

Bootstrap and simulation

Bootstrap approximates a sampling distribution by resampling observed units with replacement. It is useful for nonlinear statistics, but the resampling unit and scheme must match the design. Resample whole clusters for cluster sampling and paired records together for paired designs. Parametric simulation can explore small-sample behavior under an explicit model. Neither method solves selection bias or unobserved population tails.

Sample-size and precision planning

If SD σ is known or anticipated, mean SE is σ/√n; desired half-width h gives approximate n=(zσ/h)². For σ=12 and h=3 at 95%, n≈62. This excludes attrition and design effects. Power calculations instead target a difference and test boundary. Precision planning directly asks how narrow the estimate should be. Consider uncertainty in σ and report sensitivity analyses.

Repeated sampling intuition

Imagine drawing 1,000 samples of n=40 from a population and calculating each mean. Their histogram approximates the sampling distribution. The spread of those means is the SE; the population SD describes individual values and is larger by about √n under iid sampling. Simulation can illustrate this distinction and reveal slow convergence under heavy tails. Repetitions are a conceptual device; one study observes one sample statistic.

Worked simulation of a sampling distribution

The following simulation draws repeated samples from a skewed exponential population. Individual values remain skewed, while the sample means become more symmetric as n increases. The empirical SD of the means estimates the standard error and should be near population SD/√n (for exponential mean 1, SD 1).

set.seed(2026)
B <- 5000
n <- 40
means <- replicate(B, mean(rexp(n, rate = 1)))
c(empirical_SE = sd(means), theoretical_SE = 1 / sqrt(n))
hist(means, breaks = 30, main = "Sampling distribution of mean")

This illustration assumes independent draws and a finite variance. It does not establish that a real study’s sample is representative or independent. Heavy-tailed populations can converge slowly; dependence changes the theoretical SE.

Student t distribution

When estimating a normal population mean with unknown variance, standardize by s/√n; the statistic follows Student’s t with n−1 df, not standard normal. The t distribution has heavier tails, reflecting uncertainty from estimating σ. As df grows it approaches normal. Paired and two-sample t procedures use corresponding degrees of freedom and design-specific variance estimates.

Bootstrap and resampling

The nonparametric bootstrap samples observed units with replacement and recalculates the statistic. Its validity depends on the empirical sample approximating the target and resampling respecting independent units. For clustered data, resample clusters; for stratified samples, preserve strata; for paired data, resample pairs. Bootstrap cannot correct selection bias or unobserved extremes.

Standard error versus prediction

A confidence interval for the population mean captures uncertainty in the estimated mean. A prediction interval for a new observation adds individual residual variation and is much wider. In a normal sample, a prediction interval uses a term approximately s√(1+1/n), while the mean interval uses s/√n. Do not use SE to describe the range of future patients.

Why convergence can be slow

The CLT is asymptotic. Strong skewness, heavy tails, and rare influential observations can make the sample-mean distribution far from normal at moderate n. In a Cauchy population, variance does not exist and the usual CLT does not apply. Biomedical variables generally have finite bounds or moments, but heavy-tailed costs can still require large samples. Simulation from plausible distributions can assess finite-sample coverage.

Stratified and finite-population sampling

Stratification can lower variance when strata are internally homogeneous and sampled deliberately. Sampling without replacement from a finite population can use a finite-population correction √[(N−n)/(N−1)] when sampling fraction is appreciable. Unequal weights may reduce effective sample size. The variance formula should match the sample design; an iid CLT does not automatically justify simple standard errors.

Bootstrap validity

The ordinary nonparametric bootstrap approximates repeated sampling from the empirical distribution. It may fail for extremes, very small samples, boundary parameters, or non-smooth statistics such as maxima. Specialized bootstrap or analytic methods may be needed. More replicates reduce Monte Carlo noise, not the structural limits of the empirical sample.

Standard error under clustering

For m participants per cluster and ICC ρ, design effect D≈1+(m−1)ρ under equal cluster size. Effective sample size is approximately n/D, so a nominal sample of 400 with m=20 and ρ=.05 has effective size about 205 for a simple mean. This approximation is not a substitute for cluster-level degrees of freedom or unequal cluster-size adjustments. It shows why row count alone exaggerates precision.

Paired and stratified statistics

For a paired difference D=X−Y, Var(D)=Var(X)+Var(Y)−2Cov(X,Y). Positive within-person correlation reduces SE of the mean difference. Stratification can reduce sampling variance when design and analysis incorporate strata. Standard errors must use the covariance structure induced by design rather than independent-observation formulas.

Report the target of uncertainty

An SE can describe a mean, proportion, regression coefficient, or other estimator. Name the statistic, its target population, design, and variance method. Do not imply that sampling uncertainty includes model misspecification, missing-data bias, or measurement error unless explicitly modeled.

A numerical precision example

Suppose individual HbA1c SD is 1.2 percentage points and n=64 independent participants. SE of the mean is 1.2/8=.15. A normal-approximation 95% interval has half-width .294; a t interval uses 63 df and is slightly wider. If n quadruples to 256 with same variance, SE falls to .075. Individual SD remains about 1.2. This demonstrates why a large sample estimates the mean precisely without making individuals more similar.

sd <- 1.2
for (n in c(64, 256)) {
  se <- sd / sqrt(n)
  print(c(n = n, se = se,
          half_width = qt(.975, df = n - 1) * se))
}

The calculation assumes independent random sampling and stable variance. Clustering, nonresponse, and design weights alter uncertainty.

CLT conditions and caveats

The classical iid CLT requires finite variance and independent, identically distributed observations. Variants permit nonidentical distributions or dependence under additional regularity conditions, but convergence rates vary. Infinite-variance heavy tails violate the standard result. In practice, biomedical measurements are often bounded, but highly skewed costs and rare-event counts can still have poor normal approximations at moderate sample size.

The CLT applies to sums/means and many smooth estimators, not every statistic. Sample maxima converge to extreme-value distributions, not normal. Ratios can be unstable when denominators approach zero. Quantiles have asymptotic normality only when density at the quantile is positive and regularity holds. Choose methods for the estimator, not by invoking the CLT generically.

Finite population correction

When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size N, the variance of a sample mean includes factor (N−n)/(N−1). If 20% of a small registry is sampled, the correction can meaningfully reduce SE; if population is very large relative to n, it is negligible. This correction applies to probability sampling and does not fix nonresponse bias.

Confidence intervals and repeated coverage

A 95% confidence interval procedure covers a fixed parameter in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions. The CLT often justifies a normal critical value for a mean, but small samples may require t or exact methods. Repeatedly checking intervals and stopping when they exclude zero can invalidate fixed-sample coverage; sequential methods or confidence sequences are needed for continuous monitoring.

Simulation as a diagnostic

Simulating the sampling distribution can assess bias, coverage, and finite-sample behavior under plausible data mechanisms. Each simulated replicate should reproduce clustering, missingness, and allocation. Monte Carlo error shrinks with simulation count, but simulation only evaluates the assumed model; it does not verify that the model matches reality. Report scenario inputs and code for reproducibility.

Central limit theorem for proportions

For independent Bernoulli outcomes with probability p, sample proportion p̂ has mean p and variance p(1−p)/n. Its standardized distribution is approximately normal when both np and n(1−p) are sufficiently large. Near the boundary, normal approximation can yield impossible intervals below zero or above one. Wilson score intervals and exact binomial intervals are common alternatives. If participants are clustered, effective information is lower and the iid approximation is optimistic.

Sampling distribution versus data distribution

A skewed dataset can yield an approximately normal sample mean, while normally distributed observations can yield a nonnormal statistic after selection or nonlinear transformation. Keep the object clear: histogram of outcomes, distribution of estimator over hypothetical samples, or posterior distribution are not the same. The CLT offers a limiting approximation for certain estimators under assumptions.

Confidence interval coverage and selection

A nominal interval’s coverage applies to a prespecified procedure under its model. Selecting the largest observed effect among many outcomes and reporting its usual interval ignores selection and undercovers. Repeated interim inspection similarly changes coverage. Simultaneous intervals, selective-inference methods, or independent validation are needed for selected claims.

Simulation of coverage

A simulation study can estimate how often a proposed interval covers the truth under plausible designs. For each replicate, generate data, apply the full analysis, and record coverage and width. Include missingness, clustering, and stopping if they occur in practice. Report number of replicates and Monte Carlo error; simulation results are conditional on the scenarios and code.

set.seed(7)
B <- 2000; n <- 30; mu <- 0; sigma <- 1
covered <- replicate(B, {
  x <- rexp(n, rate = 1) - 1 # skewed population, mean zero
  ci <- t.test(x)$conf.int
  ci[1] <= mu && mu <= ci[2]
})
mean(covered)

The t interval’s coverage here can be compared across n or distributions. This code is illustrative and does not prove the CLT applies to any particular study.

Planning with desired precision

For a mean, approximate 95% half-width is 1.96σ/√n; solving gives n≈(1.96σ/h)². If σ=12 and desired half-width is 3, n≈62. This planning targets precision, unlike power planning for a specified contrast. Add attrition and design effects, and use t quantiles iteratively for small n. If σ is uncertain, evaluate scenarios and consider blinded internal variance reassessment.

Standard error and prediction interval

For a future observation, uncertainty includes individual variability and estimated-mean uncertainty. Under normal iid assumptions, a prediction interval for one new value uses s√(1+1/n), while a confidence interval for μ uses s/√n. The prediction interval remains wide as n grows because person-to-person variation persists; the mean interval shrinks. Do not present an SE as expected range for individual patients.

What the CLT does not guarantee

The CLT does not ensure unbiasedness, representative sampling, independence, or correct model specification. A precisely estimated mean from a convenience sample can target the wrong population. It does not guarantee normality of raw observations or validity of an interval after outcome selection. It is a statement about limiting behavior of an estimator under assumptions; practical inference also needs a good design and suitable variance calculation.

Use the right variance estimator

The iid standard error s/√n applies to a simple independent sample. Cluster-robust, survey-linearization, replicate-weight, or model-based SEs may be needed in other designs. The CLT can support asymptotic normality of those estimators when the number of independent units is adequate, but small numbers of clusters require special care. State the number of independent units, not only total records.

Final interpretation

Sampling distributions explain why estimates vary and how intervals are calibrated. The CLT often justifies normal approximations for means, but its assumptions and finite-sample behavior matter. Report the estimator, design-aware SE, interval method, and limitations. Do not use the theorem as a blanket defense of any large-sample analysis.

A useful mental separation

Individual variability is summarized by SD; estimator variability by SE; uncertainty limits by confidence or credible intervals; future individual variability by prediction intervals. These concepts may all use a normal curve but refer to different random quantities. Label the target every time and use the design to calculate its uncertainty.

Sample size and precision example

If SD is 12 units and desired 95% half-width is 3, n≈(1.96×12/3)²≈62 under iid assumptions. If cluster design effect is 1.8, a rough target becomes 112 individuals, with adequate numbers of clusters required. If attrition is 15%, recruit about 132. Exact planning should account for t critical values and design specifics. A precision target asks how well the mean is estimated; a power target asks about detection of a specified contrast.

Why standard errors shrink slowly

SE decreases as 1/√n. Doubling sample size reduces SE only by factor 1/√2≈.71; halving SE requires quadrupling n. Improving measurement reliability or reducing unnecessary heterogeneity can sometimes add information more efficiently than enrollment alone, provided the target estimand remains unchanged.

Summary for practice

Sampling distributions describe estimator variability under repeated data generation. The CLT often supports normal approximations for means, but dependence, heavy tails, selection, and small samples matter. Use a design-aware SE and distinguish it from SD and prediction uncertainty. Precision reflects sampling variability under assumptions, not total validity.

The reported confidence level belongs to the interval procedure; after data are observed, describe its limits as compatible values under that procedure rather than as a probability assigned to the fixed parameter.

The number of rows is not the effective sample size when observations share clusters, people, or sampling weights.

For a sample mean, report both the observed SD and design-aware SE when readers need to distinguish individual spread from estimator precision.

The CLT justifies an approximation only under its assumptions; it does not convert a biased sample into a valid population estimate.

Simulation can assess coverage under a specified data-generating process, but conclusions remain conditional on that process.

For small samples, use t, exact, permutation, or bootstrap methods only when their own design assumptions are appropriate.

Summary of sampling behavior

The standard error describes repeated-sample variability of an estimator under a design. The CLT supports useful approximations, but finite-sample convergence and dependence matter. A valid sampling distribution cannot compensate for biased recruitment or an incorrect estimand.

The sample size alone does not determine precision if observations are clustered or weighted.

Propensity matching

In observational data, sicker patients often get the treatment, so a simple comparison confuses the treatment effect with being sicker. The propensity score compresses everything you measured about treatment selection into one number — and matching on it makes the groups comparable, on what you measured.

Core idea: comparable patients, not comparable outcomes

In a randomized trial, treatment allocation is independent of confounders by design. In observational data — hospital registries, claims databases, chart reviews — sicker patients often get surgery or a new drug, so treated and untreated groups differ systematically. Comparing them directly conflates the treatment effect with the effect of being sicker. This is confounding by indication, and it is the dominant source of bias in clinical observational studies.

The propensity score is one of several tools (alongside regression adjustment and stratification) for mimicking randomization on the measured covariates. It is defined as the conditional probability that a patient receives the treatment of interest, given their observed baseline characteristics. For a binary treatment A (1 = treated, 0 = untreated) and measured covariates X:

e(x)=P(A=1∣X=x)e(x) = \mathrm{P}(A = 1 \mid X = x)e(x)=P(A=1∣X=x)

It summarizes all measured confounding information into a single number between 0 and 1 for each patient. The key theoretical result, due to Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), is that under the assumptions below, treatment and potential outcomes are conditionally independent given the propensity score:

A⊥{Y(0),Y(1)}∣e(X)A \perp \{Y(0), Y(1)\} \mid e(X)A⊥{Y(0),Y(1)}∣e(X)

In plain language: among patients with the same propensity score, treatment assignment behaves as if it were random with respect to the measured covariates. (For background, see the overview of propensity scores on Wikipedia ↗.)

The one-liner to remember: matching on the propensity score makes the groups comparable on everything you measured — nothing more, nothing less.

The two assumptions it relies on

  • No unmeasured confounding. Every variable that jointly influences both treatment selection and the outcome must be measured and included. If an important confounder is missing from X — for example, a clinician's subjective judgment of frailty that never made it into the chart — the propensity score cannot fix it.
  • Positivity (overlap, or common support). Every patient must have a nonzero chance of receiving either treatment: 0 < e(x) < 1 for all observed x. In practice, the treated and untreated propensity score distributions should meaningfully overlap, so that comparable counterparts actually exist.

What propensity matching is NOT

  • It does not remove unmeasured confounding. A match that achieves perfect balance on measured covariates can still be completely biased if an unmeasured confounder drives both treatment and outcome.
  • It is not a substitute for randomization. It mimics randomization with respect to measured covariates only.
  • It does not remove bias in a single step. You must check (further down) that balance was actually achieved; a poor model or poor overlap can silently leave large residual differences.
  • It is an analysis tool, not a data-collection recommendation. If the confounders were never measured, no analysis method can recover them.

Which covariates to match on

Covariate selection is the most practical — and most commonly mis-handled — part of a propensity-score analysis. The general principle is to include every measured covariate that predicts treatment assignment and is associated with the outcome. In the working rule of thumb used in the literature (e.g. Austin 2011; Stuart 2010):

  • Definitely include all confounders — variables that influence both who gets treated and the outcome.
  • Preferably also include outcome predictors that have little or no association with treatment. This does not bias the treatment effect estimate (conditional on the confounders) and can improve precision by reducing residual outcome variability. This is why propensity models are often deliberately over-adjusted relative to a minimal confounder set.
  • Do not include variables affected by treatment (mediators on the path from treatment to outcome) and do not include colliders (variables caused by both treatment and outcome, or by both plus some other cause).

Why the “do not include” list matters

Mediators are measured downstream of treatment. If you study a blood-pressure drug and include post-randomization blood pressure, that variable is partly caused by the drug; conditioning on it blocks part of the causal effect you are trying to measure. The propensity score is defined on baseline covariates — everything measured before treatment assignment. Colliders behave similarly: a classic example is a variable caused by both treatment and the outcome — for instance survival to a follow-up visit, or entry into a study that requires both conditions; conditioning on it induces a spurious association between variables that are otherwise independent. A practical test: if the covariate can plausibly have been changed by the treatment, it does not belong in the propensity model. When in doubt, measure the covariate before treatment and keep the timing explicit.

Two judgment calls to keep in mind: facility and year are often included in multi-site studies to absorb unmeasured site-level practice variation — a defensible convention, not a rule; socioeconomic variables belong only when they are actually measured and plausibly related to both treatment choice and outcome in the setting.

The over-adjustment nuance

The propensity score estimates treatment assignment, not the outcome. So including extra outcome predictors that are not confounders does not bias the treatment effect (under no unmeasured confounding and correct overlap) and can improve precision. But piling in dozens of weakly related variables can make the model unstable, produce extreme propensity scores (near 0 or 1), and destroy overlap. “More covariates” has diminishing returns and a real cost: err toward including relevant baseline covariates, then check the overlap histogram after estimation — extreme scores are the symptom of a model that has over-fitted noise or captured idiosyncratic treatment rules. Brookhart and colleagues' simulation study confirms this: including covariates that predict the outcome even when they barely predict treatment reduces the variance of the treatment-effect estimate without increasing its bias, whereas adding covariates that only predict treatment does the opposite — which is why a good propensity model is built for balance, not for exposure prediction (Brookhart et al. 2006).

When there are hundreds or thousands of covariates

With very large candidate sets (claims databases, EHR-derived features), plain logistic regression overfits. Standard responses include LASSO (L1-regularized logistic regression, which also performs variable selection), elastic net when predictors are correlated, machine-learning estimators such as gradient-boosted trees or random forests, and the high-dimensional propensity score (hdPS) used in pharmacoepidemiology. The pedagogical point: the covariate-selection strategy changes, but everything downstream — matching, balance checking, analysis — stays the same.

Estimating the propensity score

Logistic regression is the workhorse and the default teaching model:

logit(e)=β0+β1x1+⋯+βkxk\mathrm{logit}(e) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_klogit(e)=β0​+β1​x1​+⋯+βk​xk​

It is transparent, easy to check, and works well when the treatment rule is roughly linear in the covariates. For non-linearities and interactions, consider splines or flexible terms in the logistic model, or gradient-boosted trees and random forests. The choice of estimator affects how well overlap is achieved — not the downstream analysis plan.

Model checking: assess calibration — do predicted scores match the observed treatment proportions within score bands? The Hosmer–Lemeshow test is a classic but sample-size-sensitive check; a calibration plot is more informative. Also check whether the score actually predicts treatment: if the area under the ROC curve is near 0.5, either the treatment is close to random (good news — less confounding to control) or the model is misspecified (check).

Practical warning: the propensity model is a tool for achieving balance, not a scientific claim about which covariates “cause” treatment. Do not over-interpret its coefficients.

Positivity, overlap, and trimming

Matching can only pair patients where the two groups coexist on the propensity score scale. If a surgical patient has a score of 0.98 and no medical patient is above 0.80, there is no honest comparison for that patient — the estimate for them would be pure extrapolation.

  • Common-support restriction: keep only patients whose score lies within the range where both groups are present (for example, between the minimum treated score and the maximum untreated score, or tighter).
  • Trimming: remove extreme scores. A conventional choice is trimming below the 1st percentile and above the 99th percentile of the treated group's score distribution (Austin 2011), or trimming wherever either group has very few patients. Report how many were trimmed and on what rule.

If overlap is fundamentally poor, matching is not the right method. Your options are to accept a very narrow estimand (the effect only in a small overlap subgroup), rethink the design question (perhaps the treatment is only given to a specific subgroup — then define the study accordingly), or use a different comparison entirely. Do not force a caliper so wide that “matched” patients are clearly not comparable.

Choosing a matching method

Once the score is estimated, there are several ways to use it. Matching is the most common, but it is one member of a family of methods.

Nearest-neighbor matching

For each treated patient, find the untreated patient(s) with the closest propensity score.

  • 1:1 matching: one untreated partner per treated patient. The most efficient use of the treatment group and the simplest to explain and analyze.
  • 1:k matching (e.g. 1:3, 1:4): up to k untreated partners per treated patient. Retains more of the untreated pool and can improve precision, at the cost of a more complex analysis (see the analysis section for how variance must be handled when k > 1).

With vs. without replacement. The default in most clinical applications is without replacement: each untreated patient can be used at most once. It mirrors how you would actually pair patients and keeps the analysis straightforward. With replacement, the same untreated patient can be matched to multiple treated patients; it retains more of the treated group but produces correlated partners and can overweight a few common untreated patients. It is rarely the first choice.

Caliper matching

Without a caliper, a treated patient in a thin part of the score distribution may be forced to pair with a poorly similar untreated patient. Caliper matching fixes this: each treated patient is matched to the nearest untreated patient within a maximum distance (the caliper); if no untreated patient falls within the caliper, the treated patient is left unmatched.

caliper=0.2×SD(logit(e))\mathrm{caliper} = 0.2 \times \mathrm{SD}\big(\mathrm{logit}(e)\big)caliper=0.2×SD(logit(e))

This rule, proposed by Austin (2011) building on earlier practice, is the standard default in the propensity-matching literature. It is a rule of thumb, not a theorem — some authors use 0.1 or 0.25 SD — and the important thing is to state the caliper you used. The caliper deliberately sacrifices some treated patients (those with no close counterpart) in exchange for better within-pair similarity; expect to lose a nontrivial fraction of the sample, especially where overlap is thin. The interactive lab below shows this trade-off directly.

Exact matching

Match treated and untreated patients on one or more covariates exactly — for example, exact calendar year and facility, or exact disease stage — and do nearest-neighbor matching within each exact-match stratum. It is common when a categorical variable is a strong driver of both treatment and outcome, and it guarantees balance on the exact-match variables by construction, at the cost of potentially thin strata.

Subclassification (stratification)

Instead of pairing individuals, divide the propensity score into strata (commonly quintiles or deciles) and compare treated versus untreated within each stratum, then combine — for example, a Cox model stratified by score decile or a Mantel–Haenszel-style pooled estimate. Subclassification is simpler than matching and preserves more of the sample, but it gives rough balance at the stratum level rather than the individual level, so it is less precise for individual-level inference.

Distance metric

You can match on the raw score values, but that scale is non-linear and heteroscedastic — the difference between 0.4 and 0.5 is not the same “distance” as between 0.9 and 1.0. The more recommended choice is Mahalanobis distance on the logit of the propensity score: working on the logit (log-odds) scale linearizes the treatment-assignment relationship, and Mahalanobis distance accounts for the (un)equal variance of the logit score across the sample. Most standard software (for example R's MatchIt or CEPM) defaults to the logit score with a Mahalanobis or Euclidean metric; caliper matching on the logit score is the conventional pairing.

Matching vs. IPTW, in one paragraph. Matching answers “what is the treatment effect among patients like those who received treatment?” — it restricts to the overlap region and analyzes a selected subset. IPTW answers “what is the average treatment effect in the full sample?” — it keeps everyone and uses weights to rebalance (and is sensitive to extreme weights when the score is near 0 or 1, so truncating or stabilizing weights is standard practice). Matching is sensitive to overlap: poor overlap means a large unmatched fraction. Both require the same two assumptions. A common practical recommendation: pick your estimand first — for example, use IPTW for the full-sample average effect and matching as a sensitivity analysis focused on the treated, or vice versa.

Trade-offs to remember: matching discards unmatched patients — modest if overlap is good, large and possibly informative if overlap is poor. The matched sample is the overlap region: patients genuinely ambivalent about treatment, and the result estimates the effect in that subpopulation (closest to the ATT), not at the extremes. Report the number and characteristics of unmatched units; never quietly analyze them as if they were matched. If a large fraction is unmatched, the result is fragile and the estimand is narrow — say so.

Checking balance after matching

Matching only works if it actually equalized the groups. Always report a balance check before trusting the effect estimate. The gold-standard presentation is a balance table and a Love plot, pre- versus post-match.

Standardized mean difference (SMD)

For each covariate, the SMD compares the difference in means between treated and untreated, scaled by a common spread so every covariate sits on a common scale:

SMD=μt−μuσt2+σu22\mathrm{SMD} = \dfrac{\mu_t - \mu_u}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_t^{2} + \sigma_u^{2}}{2}}}SMD=2σt2​+σu2​​​μt​−μu​​

For a binary covariate, use the proportions in place of the means:

SMD=pt−pupt(1−pt)+pu(1−pu)2\mathrm{SMD} = \dfrac{p_t - p_u}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_t(1 - p_t) + p_u(1 - p_u)}{2}}}SMD=2pt​(1−pt​)+pu​(1−pu​)​​pt​−pu​​

This pooled-SD scaling is the conventional definition (Austin 2009, Austin 2011) and what most software reports. The conventional rule of thumb is that |SMD| < 0.1 indicates adequate balance — a difference of less than one-tenth of a standard deviation is considered trivial — while some stricter standards use |SMD| < 0.05. These are descriptive conventions, not statistical cutoffs; state which one you use.

Variance ratio

For continuous covariates, means can be balanced while the spread is not. The variance ratio is var_t / var_u; a conventional acceptable range is roughly 0.5 to 2.0 (some authors accept 0.2–5.0 — state your standard). A ratio far outside this range means the groups differ in variability even when the means match, which matters especially for skewed outcomes.

The Love plot

A Love plot (a dumbbell or slope plot) displays, for each covariate on the y-axis, the pre-match and post-match SMD on the x-axis, usually with a shaded band at |SMD| < 0.1. It shows at a glance: (a) which covariates were badly imbalanced before matching, (b) whether matching fixed them, and (c) whether matching introduced new imbalance — it can, if the caliper discarded a non-random subset. The post-match points should sit comfortably inside the 0.1 band; any that remain outside are candidates for the residual adjustment described next.

The propensity-score overlap histogram

A histogram or density of the score for treated versus untreated patients, ideally shown both before any restriction and after trimming or matching, shows how much of the sample lies in the overlap region and flags extreme-score patients who have no plausible counterpart. If the two distributions barely overlap, no matching scheme can produce a credible comparison — that is a fundamental design problem, not a tuning problem.

The critical methodological point: balance is a descriptive assessment, not a hypothesis test. Do not report p-values from t-tests or chi-square tests of covariate differences and claim balance where the p-value is large. In large samples, p-values are tiny even for trivially small differences; in small samples, they are large even for important ones. The SMD is scale-free and sample-size-free — exactly why it is the right tool.

Analysis after matching

A frequent and serious error: after 1:1 caliper matching, analysts run an ordinary unpaired t-test or unstratified logistic regression on the matched sample, treating the 2N patients as independent. They are not — the pairs are correlated by construction, so the unpaired standard error is wrong. When the pairs are similar (the usual case after good matching) it is typically too large and the test is conservative; if matching failed to balance a covariate it can be biased in either direction. It also throws away the precision the matching bought. Use a pair-aware analysis.

Analyze the matched pairs, respecting the dependence

  • 1:1 matching, continuous outcome: a paired t-test on the within-pair differences (treated − untreated), or equivalently a regression of the outcome on treatment with the pair as a fixed effect (one dummy per pair). Report the mean difference with a 95% CI and p-value — the same standard-error logic as in the sampling distributions article.
  • 1:1 matching, binary outcome: McNemar's test on the 2×2 table of pair outcomes (both had the event / only treated / only untreated / neither), or a conditional logistic regression (pair as the stratum), which gives an odds ratio.
  • 1:1 matching, time-to-event outcome: a Cox model stratified by pair, or a frailty model with the pair as the random effect.
  • 1:k matching (k > 1): the untreated partners of one treated patient are not exchangeable in the same simple way; use a cluster-robust (sandwich) variance with the treatment unit (the treated patient and its k partners) as the cluster, or a mixed model with a random intercept per treatment unit. Most matching software (e.g. MatchIt) handles the variance estimation automatically if you specify the design correctly.

Analyze the matched sample, not the full sample

The treatment effect estimate and its confidence interval apply only to the matched (overlap) sample. Do not pool unmatched patients into the primary analysis; they belong in a sensitivity analysis or a separate analysis (for example, an IPTW analysis). State the estimand explicitly — for instance, “the mean difference in outcome between treated and propensity-matched untreated patients among those with a propensity score within the caliper of a treated patient.”

Residual adjustment if balance is imperfect

If a few covariates still show |SMD| > 0.1 after matching, the usual options are: add the propensity score itself (or a spline of it) as a covariate in the final regression; add the specific residual covariate(s) that remain imbalanced (a lab value the score model under-weighted, for example); or re-estimate the score giving that covariate more weight and re-match. This is a judgment call — some reviewers prefer a pure matched comparison, others want the residual adjustment. If you adjust, re-report the balance table after the adjustment.

Reporting checklist

  1. Estimand (ATT or ATE) and the matching specification (1:1 or 1:k, caliper value, distance metric, with or without replacement).
  2. Score model: the list of covariates included and the model form (logistic, etc.).
  3. Sample accounting: N total, N treated, N untreated, N matched — and N unmatched, with their characteristics if notable.
  4. Balance table: pre- and post-match SMD (plus variance ratios for continuous covariates), ideally as a Love plot.
  5. Effect estimate with 95% CI and p-value, from a pair- or cluster-aware analysis.
  6. Sensitivity / limitations: note unmeasured-confounding risk and any trimming done.

Worked example: surgery versus medical management

The question

In a hospital registry, does surgical resection (versus medical management) improve 1-year survival among patients newly diagnosed with a localized tumor? N = 1,200: 420 had surgery, 780 received medical management.

Step 1 — Choose baseline (pre-treatment) covariates

Age, sex, tumor stage, Charlson comorbidity index, ECOG performance status, baseline hemoglobin, eGFR, prior hospitalization in the last 12 months, calendar year, and index facility. Excluded: 3-month post-treatment weight and post-operative complications — both are downstream of the treatment decision, so they belong in the outcome model at most, never in the score model.

Step 2 — Estimate the score

Logistic regression of surgery on the nine covariates. AUC = 0.78 — reasonable discrimination, which means treatment was not random and adjustment is needed. The calibration plot looks adequate.

Step 3 — Check overlap and trim

The score for surgical patients ranges 0.10–0.95; for medical patients, 0.02–0.90. There is substantial overlap, but a few surgical patients with scores above 0.90 have no medical counterpart. Trim the top 5% of the surgical score distribution (about 20 patients) to common support, leaving 400 surgical and 780 medical patients.

Step 4 — Match

1:1 nearest-neighbor, without replacement, with exact matching on calendar year and index facility, caliper = 0.2 × SD(logit score) = 0.2 × 0.55 ≈ 0.11 on the logit scale. Result: 348 matched pairs; 52 surgical patients remain unmatched because no medical patient fell within the caliper.

Step 6 — Analyze the pairs

One-year survival is binary, so use McNemar's test on the matched pairs (equivalently, conditional logistic regression). Illustrative result: 306/348 surgical survivors versus 281/348 medical survivors — a matched difference of about 7.2 percentage points, 95% CI 2.9 to 11.5, p = 0.001.

Step 7 — Interpret, with the honest caveat

Among registry patients whose score was close enough to a matched surgical counterpart (the overlap region), surgical resection was associated with 7.2 percentage points higher 1-year survival than medical management. This is not proof of causation: it assumes no unmeasured confounding (for example, unrecorded clinician judgment of operability), and the estimate generalizes to the overlap population — not to the 52 unmatched, high-severity surgical patients.

Limitations and honest caveats

  1. No protection from unmeasured confounding. The single biggest limitation. Clinician intuition about frailty, social support, or prognosis is often the real driver of treatment choice and is rarely in the chart.
  2. The matched sample limits generalizability. The estimate is for the overlap population, which may be healthier or milder than the full treated population.
  3. Balance ≠ unbiasedness. Perfect measured-covariate balance does not imply no residual bias.
  4. The score model is only as good as its covariates and its specification. A missing confounder or a badly misspecified model produces a false sense of security.
  5. Plan a sensitivity analysis. Ideas: an E-value or Rosenbaum-bounds–style analysis (how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to explain away the result?); comparing matching, IPTW, and covariate-adjustment estimates (agreement across methods is reassuring); and negative-control outcomes — outcomes the treatment should not affect — as a falsification test.

Key takeaways

  • The propensity score compresses measured confounding into P(treatment | baseline covariates); within groups of the same score, treatment behaves as if randomized with respect to what you measured.
  • Include baseline confounders (and outcome predictors for precision); never include mediators or colliders — the score model is a baseline model.
  • A common defensible default: 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement on the logit score, with a caliper of 0.2 × SD(logit score) — and state what you used.
  • Always check balance with pre- vs. post-match SMD (|SMD| < 0.1 convention), variance ratios, and an overlap histogram — descriptively, never with p-values.
  • Analyze the matched sample with pair- or cluster-aware methods: paired t-test, McNemar's test, or stratified Cox — and report the matched sample size, the balance table, and the estimate with its 95% CI.
  • Propensity methods cannot remove unmeasured confounding, and the estimate belongs to the overlap population, not the extremes. Say so.

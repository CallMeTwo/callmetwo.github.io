## Overview and key ideas

Frequentist inference asks, "if the data were generated by a fixed true effect, how likely would we see a result like this?" Bayesian inference flips the question: "given the data I have, how should my beliefs about the effect change?"

The engine is **Bayes' theorem**: the posterior distribution of a parameter is proportional to the likelihood (the data, viewed as a function of the parameter) times the prior (what you believed before seeing this data). Written loosely: *posterior ∝ likelihood × prior*. You start with a **prior distribution** over the plausible values of the unknown, multiply it by the likelihood of the observed data, and normalise to get the **posterior** — a full probability distribution for the parameter, not a single number.

Practical consequences that matter in medicine:

- The **credible interval** has a direct probability reading: "there is a 95% probability the true effect lies in this interval."
- You can **combine a new trial with earlier trials** of the same question — the prior carries the accumulated evidence.
- **Decision making is natural**: you can compute the probability that the new treatment beats the standard by more than a clinically meaningful margin.

Modern Bayesian analysis is done almost entirely by **Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**: a computer algorithm that draws a large sample from the posterior even when it has no closed form. MCMC constructs a chain of dependent draws that, after a burn-in period, explores the shape of the posterior; from those draws you read means, medians, credible intervals, and tail probabilities directly. Widely used engines include Stan and its No-U-Turn Sampler, JAGS, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo implementations in Python.

## When to use it

| Setting | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Rare-disease trial | With 40 patients, a frequentist CI is wide and uninformative; pooling a prior from 3 earlier pilot studies sharpens the posterior |
| Adaptive / dose-finding design | Update the dose selection continuously as each cohort of patients accrues, rather than at a single pre-planned analysis |
| Regulatory / meta-analysis | Synthesise several trials with a hierarchical prior to estimate a common effect and its heterogeneity |
| Early-phase oncology | Estimate the probability the response rate exceeds a threshold, informing a go/no-go decision |

Choose Bayesian when prior evidence is strong and trustworthy, when the sample is small, when the design is adaptive, or when the decision-maker wants answers framed as probabilities about the unknown.

## Assumptions and limitations

- **The prior matters when the data are weak.** With small n, a misspecified or over-confident prior can dominate the posterior. With large n, any reasonable prior is washed out by the data. Always check how much the answer moves under a range of sensible priors (sensitivity analysis).
- **Subjectivity is a feature, but it must be declared.** An "informative" prior encodes genuine prior evidence; a "vague" or "weakly informative" prior does minimal so. Neither is free — both are choices that must be stated and justified.
- **The likelihood and model must still be correct.** A wrong model with a clever prior still gives a wrong posterior (garbage in, garbage out).
- **MCMC output must be diagnosed.** Correlated chains, poor mixing, or non-convergence silently corrupt the posterior summary. Check convergence (e.g. R-hat near 1) and effective sample size before trusting any number.

## Worked example

A phase II single-arm trial tests a new drug for a rare cancer: 12 of 40 patients achieved a durable response. Suppose a Beta(6, 34) prior, with mean 0.15 and effective prior sample size 40, represents historical information about the response rate. This prior treats the historical benchmark as uncertain evidence; it is not equivalent to a randomized concurrent control.

With a binomial likelihood, conjugacy gives a Beta(18, 62) posterior: add 12 responses and 28 nonresponses to the prior parameters. Its mean is 18/80 = 0.225, its 95% equal-tail credible interval is approximately 0.141 to 0.322, and the posterior probability that the response rate exceeds 0.15 is about 0.96.

Under this specified model and prior, one can say there is about a 96% posterior probability that the response rate exceeds the 15% benchmark. The uncertainty in the benchmark itself is not modeled here; a formal comparison should model both groups and account for differences between historical and current patients.

If the decision is whether to proceed to a phase III trial, the framework extends naturally: compute the posterior probability that the response rate exceeds a pre-agreed threshold (say 25%), and pre-specify that a probability above 80% triggers continuation. That turns a fuzzy "is it promising?" discussion into a number tied to a declared decision rule.

## Interpretation and common pitfalls

- **A credible interval is not a confidence interval** — it is a probability statement about the parameter conditional on the data and the chosen prior, not the long-run coverage frequency of a procedure.
- **Priors are not "unfair advantage"** — a well-justified prior with published evidence is a legitimate input; hiding it behind a "non-informative" label is the pitfall.
- **Always report the prior** — the prior, the likelihood/model, and the software so the posterior is reproducible and auditable.
- **Do not read a posterior probability of a hypothesis directly from a point estimate** — to compare hypotheses you need prior model probabilities and the full posterior predictive, not a hand-wavy "p-value translation."

Bayesian results depend on the likelihood, prior, and model; “the data update the prior” does not make a weakly identified analysis objective. Show prior distributions on the parameter scale, justify their plausible range, and perform prior and posterior predictive checks. For a treatment effect, report posterior quantities tied to a decision (for example, P(benefit exceeds a prespecified clinically important threshold)), not only whether a credible interval excludes zero. With hierarchical models, partial pooling can stabilize small groups, but subgroup posteriors remain sensitive to the model and prior when data are sparse. MCMC convergence diagnostics are necessary computational checks, not evidence that the scientific model is correct.

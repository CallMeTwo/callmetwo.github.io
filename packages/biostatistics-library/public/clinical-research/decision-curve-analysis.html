Decision curve analysis

AUC tells you whether a model ranks patients well. Decision curve analysis asks the question that leads to action: does actually using the model's predicted risks — treating when the prediction exceeds a threshold — beat the two no-model strategies, treating everyone or treating no one?

Core idea: from prediction to decisions

A clinical prediction model is usually graded on two qualities. Discrimination — summarized by the AUC — asks whether the model ranks patients well: do the sicker patients tend to get higher predicted probabilities than the healthier ones? Calibration asks whether the predicted probabilities themselves are accurate: of everyone the model labels “20% risk,” do about 20% actually go on to have the event?

Both of those answer the question “does this model predict well?” Decision curve analysis (DCA) answers a different, decision-oriented question: if a clinician actually used this model's predicted risks to decide who gets treated, would that beat the two obvious simpler strategies — treating everyone, or treating no one — at a given threshold probability? The method is from Vickers & Elkin 2006, with the net-benefit framework developed further in Vickers 2016 and a short plain-language account in Fitzgerald et al. 2015.

Set up the decision. You have a binary outcome — the condition you want to act on. You have a treatment or action that helps a person who has the condition but harms or costs a person who does not (an unnecessary, burdensome intervention). A model predicts each patient's probability of the condition, p̂. The decision rule is treat if p̂ > t, where t is a threshold probability — the predicted risk above which you would choose to act.

The right value of t is not a statistical constant. It depends on the patient's or decision-maker's trade-off between the benefit of treating and the harm of treating — the consequence weights. A clinician managing a dangerous, easily fatal condition will accept a lower threshold (treat more liberally) than a clinician offering a risky or intrusive treatment for a mild outcome. DCA does not force you to pick a single threshold. Instead it traces the clinical value of the model across the whole range of thresholds, so the reader can see at which trade-off points using the model helps, and at which points it does not. (For background, see the overview of decision curve analysis on Wikipedia ↗.)

The one-liner to remember: DCA is a complement to discrimination and calibration, not a replacement. AUC and calibration ask whether the model predicts; DCA asks whether using it to decide beats treating everyone — or no one. A model can be a fine discriminator (good AUC) yet useless in practice if, at the thresholds a clinician would actually use, it does not beat the no-model defaults.

Net benefit, and how to read a decision curve

Net benefit is the single quantity DCA plots. At threshold probability t, the model's net benefit is

NB(t)=p TPR(t)−(1−p) FPR(t) t1−t\mathrm{NB}(t) = p\,\mathrm{TPR}(t) - (1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}(t)\,\dfrac{t}{1-t}NB(t)=pTPR(t)−(1−p)FPR(t)1−tt​

where p is the condition (disease) prevalence in the analysis cohort; TPR(t) is the model's sensitivity (true-positive rate) when you apply the decision rule “treat if p̂ > t”; and FPR(t) is the model's false-positive rate at that same threshold. Read the formula as: expected true positives, minus expected false positives, where each false positive is discounted (or amplified) by t/(1−t) relative to a false negative. The first term, p·TPR(t), is the fraction of all patients who are correctly treated — the good. The second term, (1−p)·FPR(t)·t/(1−t), is the fraction of all patients who are wrongly treated — the bad, weighted by how costly a false positive is at that threshold.

Where the t/(1−t) weight comes from

The threshold t is the disease probability at which you are indifferent between treating and not treating a single patient. Imagine one patient whose true disease probability is exactly t. If you treat them, you harm them only in the (1−t) chance they are healthy — one false positive; if you do not treat them, you harm them only in the t chance they are diseased — one false negative. At the indifference point those two harms balance: (1−t) × (false-positive harm) = t × (false-negative harm), so

a false positive is worth t/(1−t) false-negative units. That is the content of the original method's equal treatment–testing harm assumption: you need not commit to separate benefit and harm numbers up front — sweeping t across the curve implicitly explores the whole range of benefit/harm trade-offs, with t = 0.5 being the special point where a false positive and a false negative cost the same. (Presented as intuition for where the weight comes from, not as a claim about the original authors' formal derivation.)

The two benchmark strategies

Two obvious strategies require no model at all, and they give the decision curve its reference lines. Treat everyone:

NBall(t)=p−(1−p) t1−t=p−t1−t\mathrm{NB}_{\mathrm{all}}(t) = p - \dfrac{(1-p)\,t}{1-t} = \dfrac{p - t}{1 - t}NBall​(t)=p−1−t(1−p)t​=1−tp−t​

everyone is treated, so the “true-positive rate” is just the prevalence p and the false-positive rate is 1−p. Treat no one has net benefit exactly 0 at every threshold.

The shape of the three lines

  • The model curve starts at p when t → 0 — at a near-zero threshold you treat essentially everyone, so the rule degenerates into treat-all and inherits its net benefit. In this lab it then declines monotonically into negative territory — as t rises you treat fewer patients, and the true positives you lose outweigh the false positives you shed; that always-falling line is a property of this closed-form benchmark, where FPR(t) = 1 − t falls exactly linearly with t. A real model's curve often looks different: because its predicted risks cluster below the cohort average, a real decision curve typically starts below p, arcs upward to an interior peak (selectively treating the highest-risk captures most true positives while shedding many false positives early), and only then declines — so in a published curve, that peak is the shape to look for.
  • Treat-all starts at p and curves downward as t rises: you are treating a fixed everyone, but the relative penalty of the false positives grows. It hits 0 exactly at t = p and goes negative beyond that.
  • Treat-none is flat at 0 for all t.

The useful range

The model is useful at threshold t when it beats both benchmarks: NB_model(t) > NB_all(t) and NB_model(t) > NB_none(t) = 0. The set of such thresholds is the useful range. To read a real curve, find where the three lines cross: the horizontal span where the model line sits above both the treat-all line and the zero line is where the model adds genuine value. Above (or outside) the useful range, using the test does more harm than good — you would be better off simply treating everyone, or simply treating no one.

Distinguishing commonly-confused ideas

DCA vs AUC / ROC

The AUC measures ranking, and it is independent of prevalence and independent of treatment consequences — it only requires that higher risk goes with higher predicted probability. DCA is prevalence-dependent, consequence-aware, and threshold-specific. The practical consequences:

  • A model can have a respectable AUC yet no useful range at the thresholds a clinician actually uses.
  • Two models with identical AUC can have different decision curves — different calibration, or different behavior in the threshold region that matters.
  • Conversely, a lower-AUC model can be more useful inside a narrow threshold window if its calibration and threshold-region behavior are better there.

AUC and DCA answer different questions and can disagree — report both.

DCA vs calibration

DCA uses predicted probabilities as-is, so miscalibration distorts the curve: a consistently over-predicting model behaves as if it were being applied at higher thresholds than intended — its curve is effectively shifted along the threshold axis. Good discrimination alone is not enough; the predicted probabilities must be well calibrated for the decision curve to be meaningful (on the calibration hierarchy, from utopia to empirical data: Van Calster et al. 2016; on appropriate validation: Steyerberg & Harrell 2016).

DCA vs “clinical utility” or “significance”

DCA is one operational definition of clinical utility, based specifically on net benefit. It is not the same thing as statistical significance (a p-value), and it is not a single risk cut-off. “Clinically useful” in everyday language can mean many things; DCA pins it down as beats the no-model benchmarks on net benefit across thresholds.

Net benefit vs accuracy or sensitivity

Net benefit is not a measure of classification accuracy. It is a decision metric in which false positives and false negatives are weighted by the clinical threshold (via t/(1−t)). A strategy can have mediocre sensitivity yet high net benefit at the thresholds that matter — and vice versa.

Assumptions and limitations

Consequence weights: the equal-harm assumption and its generalization

The base method does not ask you to commit to separate benefit and harm numbers: it implicitly treats the harm of treating a healthy person and the harm of missing a diseased person as comparable, and lets the threshold sweep encode the trade-off (with t/(1−t) as the price of a false positive in false-negative units). If the consequences are not comparable, you can make the weights explicit. The decision rule becomes: treat when the expected benefit of treating a patient with disease probability p exceeds the expected loss of wrongly treating a healthy one,

p BH>(1−p) BLp\,B_{H} > (1-p)\,B_{L}pBH​>(1−p)BL​

and solving for the disease probability at which you would flip from treat to do-not-treat gives the consequence-weighted threshold:

t∗=BLBH+BLt^{*} = \dfrac{B_{L}}{B_{H} + B_{L}}t∗=BH​+BL​BL​​

where BH is the net benefit of treating a diseased patient and BL the net loss of treating a healthy one (the false-positive penalty). The threshold is the ratio of the false-positive penalty to the sum of both consequence weights, so a high cost of wrongly treating a healthy person pushes t* up, and a high benefit of catching a case pushes it down. (Presented as the generalization with explicit weights; the base curve corresponds to the special case in which the two kinds of harm are comparable.)

The rest of the assumptions

  1. Constant consequences across patients. DCA assumes the treatment's benefit/loss is the same for every patient. With real heterogeneity in consequences, the single curve is only an average over the cohort, not a personalized statement.
  2. Prevalence dependence. The curves and the useful range depend on the cohort prevalence p used in the analysis. Applying the same model to a different-prevalence population changes the decision — the treat-all line moves and the model curve rescales. Do not transport a decision curve across prevalence without re-evaluation.
  3. Calibration dependence and validation. Because DCA uses predicted probabilities directly, it is sensitive to miscalibration (see above). And a decision curve computed on an unvalidated, development-only model is optimistic — validate (internal, internal–external, or external) before drawing conclusions (Steyerberg & Harrell 2016).
  4. Threshold selection can be gamed. One can always pick the one threshold where the model looks best. The honest report is the full curve with its useful range, not a single cherry-picked threshold.
  5. Test cost and harm are not explicit. The original formulation does not separately model the cost or harm of the test itself, unless you fold it into the consequence weights.

Worked example: 30-day heart-failure readmission

The question

A clinical prediction score estimates the probability of readmission within 30 days of hospital discharge for heart failure. The decision: enroll the patient in an intensive follow-up program (home monitoring plus a nurse visit), or not.

Cohort: N = 10,000 discharged patients; D = 1,000 (10%) were readmitted within 30 days (prevalence p = 0.10); H = 9,000 were not. The score's AUC = 0.70.

Step 1 — Build the benchmark

The standard teaching benchmark: diseased patients have latent score Y ~ N(μ, 1), non-diseased patients Y ~ N(0, 1), and the model predicts p̂ = Φ(Y) (Φ = the standard normal CDF, as in the sampling distributions article). Under this model the AUC is Φ(μ/√2), so for AUC = 0.70:

μ = √2·Φ⁻¹(0.70) = 0.741614. A key property: because “treat if p̂ > t” is exactly “treat if Y > Φ⁻¹(t)”, the score's operating characteristics at threshold t are determined by the AUC and the prevalence alone — TPR(t) = Φ(μ − Φ⁻¹(t)), FPR(t) = 1 − t exactly, and NB_model(t) = p·TPR(t) − (1−p)·t. That is what makes every number below reproducible from just two inputs.

Step 3 — Read the curve

  • The model is useful only for threshold probabilities below ≈ 0.11. The exact useful range at AUC 0.70 and 10% prevalence is t ≈ 0.013 to t ≈ 0.108 — the only span where NB_model exceeds both benchmarks (the two roots of NB_model − NB_all and of NB_model − 0 are ≈ 0.0125 and ≈ 0.1084).
  • At t = 0.05 the model edges out treat-everyone (0.054 vs 0.053). It wins at all only because it spares 450 of the 9,000 healthy patients (enrolling 8,550 instead of 9,000) while still catching 991 of the 1,000 readmissions — missing only 9.
  • At t = 0.15 the best policy is to treat no one: NB_model = −0.039 and NB_treat-all = −0.059 are both worse than 0. An AUC of 0.70 sounds good, but once you would only act on patients whose predicted risk exceeds 15%, the false positives the score produces outweigh the readmissions it catches.
Clearly illustrative: this is a constructed benchmark, not a real trial or dataset. Its arithmetic is exactly reproducible from the AUC and prevalence alone — and that is the pedagogical point. A real DCA is computed from an actually validated model's predicted probabilities, and the useful range would shift with the real (not assumed) prevalence and calibration.

Practical takeaways

  • Report the full curve, not one threshold. Show net benefit across the whole threshold range with both benchmarks; a single threshold is easy to cherry-pick.
  • State the prevalence you used. Curves and the useful range are prevalence-dependent; a reader in a different-prevalence population must know the basis.
  • Check calibration before running DCA. Miscalibration shifts the curve along the threshold axis and can mislead about where the model is useful.
  • Know your consequence trade-off. Identify the threshold probability that matches the treatment's benefit/harm balance — that is the part of the curve to lean on when making the decision.
  • Compare against BOTH treat-all and treat-none. Beating only one benchmark is not enough; usefulness requires beating both.
  • Do not run DCA on an unvalidated model. Development-sample curves are optimistic; validate (internal / internal–external / external) first.
  • Remember DCA complements AUC and calibration — it does not replace them. Report discrimination, calibration, and clinical value together.

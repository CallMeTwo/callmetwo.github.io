Quantiles and the interquartile range

A quantile is a value defined by position in the ordered data, not by averaging — and that is exactly why the median and the interquartile range survive outliers that overwhelm the mean and the standard deviation. This page covers quantiles and quartiles, the nine interpolation conventions, the Tukey boxplot and its 1.5×IQR fences, and a live lab in which a few extreme values drag the mean and SD while the box barely moves.

The core idea: position, not averaging

A quantile of a data set is a value that cuts off a specified fraction of the observations. The p-th quantile (for p between 0 and 1) is the value below which a proportion p of the data fall. Quantiles are defined by position in the ordered data, not by averaging — which is exactly why they behave so well in the presence of outliers. (For a general background, see the Wikipedia article on quantiles ↗.)

The special cases everyone uses: the median is the 50th percentile, the p = 0.5 quantile — half the data are below it, half above. The quartiles divide the data into four equal-probability groups: Q1 = the 25th percentile, Q2 = the 50th percentile (the median), and Q3 = the 75th percentile.

There is no single canonical way to turn a finite sorted sample into “the” p-th quantile: for most p the desired cut point falls between two data values, and you have to decide how to interpolate. Hyndman and Fan catalog nine standard definitions, R-1 through R-9, that differ in how the quantile position is placed on the integer grid [1]. This page uses R-7, the linear-interpolation method that is the default in R, NumPy, and most other software:

h = (n − 1)·p,   j = ⌊h⌋,   q̂(p) = x_(j+1) + (h − j)·(x_(j+2) − x_(j+1)),   with x_(1) ≤ x_(2) ≤ … ≤ x_(n)

The median is q̂(0.5), Q1 = q̂(0.25), and Q3 = q̂(0.75). The fact that nine conventions exist is not a curiosity — it is the reason two statistical packages can print slightly different quartiles for the same data.

The IQR and the boxplot

The interquartile range (IQR) is the span of the middle half of the data. It is a measure of spread that ignores the outer quarter of the data on each side, which is precisely what makes it robust: because the IQR is built from quantiles (positions) rather than from squared deviations, one extreme value changes it very little.

IQR = Q3 − Q1

The boxplot [2] is the five-number summary — the minimum, Q1, the median, Q3, and the maximum — drawn to scale. The “box” runs from Q1 to Q3 (its height is the IQR) with a line at the median; the “whiskers” extend to the most extreme observations still inside the outlier fences; and observations beyond the fences are plotted individually as outliers. The whole display is a single glance at center (the median), spread (the IQR), asymmetry (where the median sits inside the box, and how long the two whiskers are), and extremes (the flagged points).

Distinguishing commonly confused ideas

Quantile vs percentile — same idea, two names

A percentile is just a quantile expressed on a 0–100 scale: the 25th percentile is the 0.25 quantile, and the 90th percentile is the 0.90 quantile. In everyday and clinical writing “percentile” is more common (a baby “in the 75th percentile for weight”); in the statistics literature “quantile” and the p ∈ (0,1) notation are more common. They are not two different objects.

IQR vs range vs SD — different notions of spread, very different robustness

The range (max − min) and the standard deviation both measure spread, but the three are not equally robust:

  • The range uses the two most extreme values, so a single extreme observation moves the range by its full magnitude. Zero robustness to a single outlier.
  • The variance/SD is built from squared deviations from the mean. Squaring means a large deviation is amplified quadratically, and because the center (the mean) itself shifts toward the outlier, a single extreme value can inflate the SD dramatically. In the worked example below, one value (13.8) inflates the SD from 0.62 to 1.92 — about 3×.
  • The IQR uses the 25th and 75th percentiles, which are immune to one extreme value. It is the robust spread to pair with the robust center (the median).

The “SD is squaring, so it is very sensitive to one extreme” point is the algebraic reason the mean/SD pair is fragile while the median/IQR pair is not.

Median vs mean — the breakdown point

The mean and the median both summarize “center,” but they have completely different robustness. The breakdown point is the fraction of the data you can corrupt (push to arbitrary values) before a statistic is dragged to an arbitrary location; Huber and Ronchetti develop this formalism [6]. The mean has breakdown point 0: a single arbitrarily large value, no matter how far out, moves the mean to match it — with n observations, one value of M shifts the mean by M/n, and as M → ∞ so does the mean. The median has breakdown point about 50%: you must corrupt roughly half the sample before the median itself can be pushed to an arbitrary location. This is the single cleanest way to say why the median is “robust” and the mean is not.

The many sample-quantile conventions

As noted in the core idea, there is no unique “the” sample quantile. Hyndman and Fan’s nine methods (R-1 … R-9) give slightly different Q1/Q3 on the same data, and on small samples the differences can be visible to a decimal place [1]. This is a convention, not a deep truth — which is why you must know which method your software uses (R-7 here) so your reported quartiles are reproducible. Two tools disagreeing is a feature of the ambiguity, not a bug in either.

Inner fence (1.5×IQR) vs outer fence (3×IQR) — mild vs extreme outliers

The boxplot flags points beyond fences built from the quartiles and the IQR:

  • Inner fence = Q1 − 1.5·IQR (low side) and Q3 + 1.5·IQR (high side). Points beyond these are mild (suspected) outliers — the standard Tukey boxplot whiskers stop at the most extreme data point inside the inner fence.
  • Outer fence = Q1 − 3·IQR and Q3 + 3·IQR. Points beyond these are extreme outliers.

Hoaglin and Iglewicz [3] and Iglewicz and Hoaglin [4] formalize this resistant (Tukey-style) labeling: the 1.5×IQR and 3×IQR multipliers are conventions for separating “mild” from “extreme,” not derived constants — rule-of-thumb values that work well for roughly normal data but are not statistically mandated.

lower fence = Q1 − 1.5·IQR,   upper fence = Q3 + 1.5·IQR

Assumptions and limitations

  1. Quantiles need ordered (and, in practice, continuous) data. The definition is “the value at position h in the ordered sample,” so the data must be orderable. For heavily discrete or tied data (a Likert scale, a lab value reported to one decimal), the value below which p falls is not unique — many observations share it — and the interpolated quantile is only a convention. Quantiles are still valid, but interpret a quantile on discrete data as “the data value at that position,” not a precise cut point.
  2. The IQR only summarizes the middle. The IQR (and the boxplot box) says nothing about the tails beyond Q1 and Q3. Two samples can have identical Q1, median, and Q3 yet wildly different tails. The IQR is deliberately blind to the extremes — that is what makes it robust, but it also means you must look at the flagged outliers and the min/max separately to see what the tails are doing.
  3. The fence is a heuristic, not a test. The 1.5×IQR / 3×IQR fences are not a significance test and not a likelihood decision; they flag candidates. A point beyond the fence is a prompt to investigate (possible assay error, unit mislabel, a legitimate extreme), not an automatic instruction to delete it.
  4. The fence is itself sample-sensitive. Because the fence is built from Q1, Q3 and the IQR — all computed from the same sample — removing or adding an extreme value changes the fence, which can change which points are flagged. The worked example exposes this directly: with the extreme 13.8 present, the only flagged outlier is 13.8; remove 13.8 and the upper fence drops to 7.075, which then flags 7.2. This is a limitation, not a bug — the outlier set you report depends on the sample you started with, so state your rule (and that it is conventional) explicitly.
  5. Sample quantiles are consistent, but any one method is a convention. For any fixed distribution, the R-7 sample quantile converges to the true population quantile as n → ∞, so all reasonable methods agree in large samples. In small samples the choice of method matters, and quartiles themselves are noisy — the standard error of a sample quantile shrinks only as 1/√n, and the quartiles (which sit at 25%/75%) are estimated less precisely than the median. Do not over-read a quartile computed from n = 10.

Worked example: HbA1c in 20 outpatients (right-skewed, with one extreme)

Setting

HbA1c (%) in 20 adult diabetes outpatients. HbA1c is right-skewed, and this sample carries one extreme value (13.8) that is a probable mislabelled/failed assay or a new-onset patient — the exact situation that motivates reporting the median (IQR) rather than the mean (SD). Sorted values (n = 20):

4.7, 4.9, 5.0, 5.1, 5.2, 5.2, 5.3, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6, 5.6, 5.7, 5.8, 5.9, 6.0, 6.1, 6.3, 6.6, 7.2, 13.8

Quantile positions (R-7)

With n = 20, the (n − 1)·p positions are:

  • Q1: (20 − 1)·0.25 = 4.75 → Q1 = x_(5) + 0.75·(x_(6) − x_(5)) = 5.2 + 0.75·(5.2 − 5.2) = 5.200
  • Median: (20 − 1)·0.50 = 9.5 → median = x_(10) + 0.5·(x_(11) − x_(10)) = 5.6 + 0.5·(5.6 − 5.6) = 5.600
  • Q3: (20 − 1)·0.75 = 14.25 → Q3 = x_(15) + 0.25·(x_(16) − x_(15)) = 6.0 + 0.25·(6.1 − 6.0) = 6.025

Five-number summary and fences

  • min = 4.7, max = 13.8
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 6.025 − 5.2 = 0.825
  • Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5·IQR = 5.2 − 1.2375 = 3.9625
  • Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5·IQR = 6.025 + 1.2375 = 7.2625
  • Flagged outlier = {13.8} — the only value beyond a fence
  • Whisker ends (most extreme data still inside the fences): lower 4.7, upper 7.2

Mean and SD (the fragile pair): mean = 6.045, SD = 1.9228.

s = √( Σ(xi − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

The single extreme 13.8 raises the mean by +0.408 (6.045 → 5.6368 gap) and inflates the SD from 0.6211 to 1.9228 (~3.1×) — both because the mean averages in the outlier and the SD squares it. Yet it leaves the median exactly at 5.600 and does not move Q1 at all, and only nudges Q3 and the IQR a little. That is robustness, concretely: the median/IQR pair tells a stable story about the typical patient while the mean/SD pair is dominated by one questionable measurement. For a skewed, long-tailed lab value like HbA1c, median (IQR) is the honest summary.

Honest limitation (the fence wrinkle). Removing 13.8 shifts Q3 to 5.950 and the upper fence to 7.075, which then flags 7.2 as the new outlier. So the set of “outliers” is not an intrinsic property of the data — it depends on the sample, because the fence is computed from that same sample. Keep this in view: the fence flags candidates to investigate, it does not make a final call, and its own position moves when the extreme values move.

Practical takeaways

  • Report median (IQR) for skewed / long-tailed health data. For right-skewed measures like HbA1c, biomarkers, lengths of stay, or costs, the distribution is not normal and the mean/SD are misleading; the STROBE statement endorses median (IQR) as the appropriate descriptive summary for non-normally distributed data [5]. This is a reporting convention — label it as such, and note it is not a derived rule.
  • Pair the median with the IQR, not just the SD. The SD is the fragile partner (it squares deviations, so one extreme inflates it). The IQR is the robust spread that matches the robust center. Reporting “median (IQR)” lets readers see a center and a spread that are both resistant to outliers.
  • The boxplot is the five-number summary, drawn to scale. Use it as the default display for a single skewed variable: center (median), spread (IQR box), asymmetry (median position + whisker lengths), and extremes (flagged points) in one figure [2].
  • The fence flags candidates — investigate, do not auto-delete. A point beyond 1.5×IQR is a hypothesis (“is this an error?”), not a verdict. Check the source: unit error, mislabel, repeat the assay, confirm the value. Auto-deleting based on a fence is how you remove the very extremes that are scientifically interesting.
  • Know your software’s quantile method (R-7 here). Quartiles differ slightly across packages because of the nine Hyndman-and-Fan conventions [1]. State or assume R-7 (the default in R and NumPy) so your reported Q1/Q3/median are reproducible, and so a reader comparing to another tool understands any small discrepancy.
